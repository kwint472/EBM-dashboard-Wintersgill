## Study Selection Criteria

### Inclusion Criteria

**Study Design (Priority Order):**
1. **Intervention studies** - Highest priority for testing leadership training effectiveness (e.g., pre-post designs, quasi-experimental)
2. **Cross-sectional studies** - For examining leadership-burnout associations at practice/organizational level
3. **Longitudinal studies** - For tracking burnout changes over time (if available)
4. **Mixed-methods studies** - For combining quantitative outcomes with qualitative understanding of mechanisms

**Population Requirements:**
- Healthcare workers (physicians, nurses, clinical/nonclinical staff) in direct patient care or support roles
- Adult healthcare professionals (18+ years)
- U.S. healthcare settings (hospitals, primary care practices, health systems)
- Sample size ≥ 50 participants for adequate statistical power (except intervention studies with strong effects)

**Temporal Requirements:**
- **COVID-19 era studies:** Published 2020-2025, data collected 2020-2024 (matches FEVS elevation period)
- **Pre-COVID baseline:** Published 2015-2020, data collected 2015-2019 (for comparison)

**Content Requirements:**
- Primary outcomes: Burnout, turnover intention, job satisfaction, or professional fulfillment measured quantitatively
- Leadership focus: Leadership interventions, leadership behaviors, organizational support, or practice culture as predictor variables
- Statistical analysis: Regression, correlation, pre-post comparison, or group differences with effect sizes and significance testing
- Full-text access available

### Exclusion Criteria
- Non-empirical studies (opinion pieces, editorials, commentary, theoretical frameworks)
- Qualitative-only studies without quantitative outcomes
- Non-peer reviewed sources (grey literature, dissertations, conference abstracts)
- Studies outside healthcare settings or without healthcare worker samples
- Non-U.S. populations (unless large multinational studies including U.S. data)
- Studies with n < 50 unless intervention studies with very strong effects
- Studies not measuring burnout, turnover, or related well-being outcomes
- Studies not examining leadership or organizational factors

## Quality Appraisal Framework

### Evidence Hierarchy (Adapted Peterson Framework)

**Level 1 - Highest Quality:**
- Randomized controlled trials of leadership interventions
- Quasi-experimental intervention studies with control/comparison groups

**Level 2 - High Quality:**
- Pre-post intervention studies without control groups but with strong effects
- Large-scale cross-sectional studies (N>500) with practice/organizational-level analysis

**Level 3 - Moderate Quality:**
- Cross-sectional studies with individual-level analysis and validated measures
- Mixed-methods studies combining quantitative + qualitative data

**Level 4 - Lower Quality (excluded from final synthesis):**
- Case studies, single-site reports
- Studies without validated measures or appropriate statistical analysis

### Study Quality Assessment Criteria

**For each selected study, evaluate:**

**1. Methodological Rigor:**
- ✓ Sample size adequate for analyses performed (power consideration)
- ✓ Validated burnout measurement instruments (MBI, single-item measures validated against MBI)
- ✓ Appropriate statistical methods (regression for predictors, logistic regression for binary outcomes, pre-post comparisons)
- ✓ Control for confounding variables (demographics, work setting, COVID-19 exposure)
- ✓ Response rate >40% for surveys (to minimize non-response bias)

**2. Relevance Assessment:**
- ✓ Population matches federal healthcare workforce (physicians, nurses, clinical/nonclinical staff)
- ✓ Burnout/turnover outcomes directly relevant to FEVS crisis (32% burnout elevation, 28% turnover elevation)
- ✓ Temporal alignment with COVID-19 period (2020-2024)
- ✓ Leadership interventions or organizational factors actionable by federal agencies

**3. Risk of Bias Evaluation:**
- **Selection bias:** Random sampling preferred; convenience sampling acceptable if large and diverse
- **Measurement bias:** Validated instruments required; self-report acceptable for burnout (inherently subjective)
- **Common method bias:** All self-report from single survey increases risk; multi-source data preferred
- **Intervention fidelity:** For intervention studies, training/implementation described and monitored
- **Attrition bias:** For pre-post studies, dropout rates <50% preferred

## Selection Criteria

### Inclusion Criteria

**Study design requirements:**
- Cross-sectional studies examining leadership-burnout relationships
- Pre-post intervention studies testing leadership training effectiveness
- Mixed-methods studies combining quantitative burnout outcomes with qualitative insights
- Studies with adequate statistical power (N≥50 for cross-sectional; N≥10 for intervention with strong effects)

**Population/context requirements:**
- Healthcare workers (physicians, nurses, clinical staff, nonclinical staff) in U.S. settings
- Direct patient care or healthcare support roles
- Multiple healthcare disciplines represented
- COVID-19 era studies (2020-2024) plus pre-COVID baseline (2015-2019)

**Outcome measures:**
- Burnout measured quantitatively (validated instruments like MBI or validated single-item measures)
- Turnover intention, job satisfaction, or professional fulfillment as secondary outcomes
- Leadership effectiveness, organizational support, or practice culture as predictors
- Statistical relationships reported (correlations, regression coefficients, odds ratios, effect sizes)

**Quality thresholds:**
- Peer-reviewed publication in academic journal
- Clear methodology and statistical analysis described
- Validated measurement instruments or well-justified measures
- Results generalizable to healthcare workforce

### Exclusion Criteria

**Study limitations:**
- Case studies or qualitative-only studies without quantitative outcomes
- Sample size <50 unless intervention study with exceptionally strong effects
- Studies without validated burnout measures or clear outcome definitions
- No statistical analysis or significance testing reported

**Context mismatches:**
- Non-healthcare populations (business, education, general workforce)
- Studies outside United States or without U.S. participants
- Executive/CEO burnout (not frontline workers)
- Student populations (medical/nursing students, not practicing professionals)

**Quality concerns:**
- Non-peer reviewed sources (grey literature, dissertations, conference abstracts, white papers)
- Opinion pieces, editorials, commentary, or theoretical frameworks without empirical data
- Program descriptions without outcome evaluation
- Studies not addressing leadership or organizational factors

**Accessibility issues:**
- Full text not available through institutional access
- Abstract-only publications without sufficient detail for quality assessment

## Challenges and Limitations

### Search Limitations

**Limited intervention studies available:**
- Only 1 true intervention study found (Study 3: leadership training)
- Most evidence from cross-sectional observational studies
- No randomized controlled trials of leadership interventions in healthcare burnout
- Limits ability to make definitive causal claims

**Federal healthcare context gap:**
- No studies specifically conducted in federal healthcare agencies (VHA, HHS, CMS)
- All studies from private/non-profit healthcare settings
- Generalizability to federal context requires assumption of similar mechanisms

**Pre-post COVID comparison limited:**
- Only 1 pre-COVID study (Study 5: 2015-2017)
- Most evidence from COVID-19 period (2020-2021)
- Difficult to isolate pandemic-specific vs. general leadership effects

### Resource Constraints

**Database access:**
- Limited to PubMed/MEDLINE and PsycINFO through institutional access
- Business Source Complete not fully searched
- Some full-text articles behind paywalls (relied on available abstracts)

**Time constraints:**
- Search conducted November 2024
- Focused on high-priority studies meeting strict criteria
- May have missed relevant studies published after search date

**Single reviewer:**
- One person conducted search, screening, and extraction
- Increases risk of missed studies or extraction errors
- Ideally would have second reviewer for validation
